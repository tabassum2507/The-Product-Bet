---
title: "Guardrails are a product decision before they're an engineering one"
date: 2026-08-13T21:26:00+05:30
tags: ["ai-pm", "product-management"]
description: "I kept deferring guardrail decisions to engineering as if they were purely technical, until one shipped a default I hadn't actually chosen."
---

An engineer asked me, almost as an aside in standup, what the model should do if a user on the health assistant asked for a specific medication dosage. I said "just make it refuse anything that sounds like medical advice," and moved on, treating it as obvious. He came back a week later having implemented a refusal so broad it also blocked questions like "is it normal for a two-year-old to nap twice a day," which is not medical advice by any reasonable definition, it's the kind of question the whole product existed to answer. The guardrail wasn't wrong given what I'd told him. What I'd told him wasn't actually a decision, it was a vibe, and he'd had to turn my vibe into an actual boundary himself.

That's the pattern I keep seeing with guardrails, they get treated as an engineering implementation detail, a safety filter someone bolts on, when the actual hard part is a product and policy question: where exactly is the line, who decided it, and what does the product do at the edge of that line instead of just inside or outside it. A refusal isn't binary in practice even though it's often implemented that way. There's a difference between refusing and redirecting, between "I can't help with that" and "I can't give you a specific dosage, but here's when you should call your pediatrician," and choosing between those is a product decision with real consequences for how useful and how trusted the thing feels.

The tradeoff that's easy to underweight is that tighter guardrails reduce risk but also reduce usefulness, and that tradeoff isn't symmetric across use cases. A support chatbot being slightly over-cautious costs you a frustrated user who has to escalate to a human. A health assistant being slightly over-cautious might cost you nothing, or it might cost you the one time a genuinely urgent question got a deflection instead of a "call emergency services now," which is a much worse failure in the other direction. I don't think there's a universal right amount of caution, it has to be set per domain, deliberately, by someone who's actually thought about what a false refusal costs versus what a false permission costs, and that someone should be the PM, not whoever happened to implement the filter that week.

What I do differently now is write actual guardrail specs before engineering builds anything, specific scenarios with the exact desired behavior, refuse, redirect, answer with a caveat, escalate to human, rather than a one-line instruction I assume is unambiguous. And for measuring whether the guardrails are calibrated right, I look at two numbers together, not one, the rate of harmful content that slipped through and the rate of legitimate questions that got refused. Optimizing only the first number is how you end up blocking questions about toddler nap schedules.
