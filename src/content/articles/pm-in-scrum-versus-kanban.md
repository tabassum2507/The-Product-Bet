---
title: "What a PM actually does in Scrum versus Kanban"
date: 2026-08-06T22:17:00+05:30
tags: ["product-management", "technical-pm"]
description: "I could define Scrum and Kanban fine, I just couldn't say what changes about my own job when a team switches between them."
---

A team I was joining had just switched from Scrum to Kanban, and in the handoff notes someone had written "PM cadence unchanged." I believed that for about two weeks before realizing almost everything about how I should be operating had actually changed, I just hadn't noticed because nobody told me to look.

In Scrum, my job is heavily front-loaded around the sprint boundary. Backlog refinement before the sprint, a defensible sprint goal going in, protecting scope during the sprint because the team committed to something specific, then a review and retro at the end where the cadence forces a checkpoint whether anyone feels ready for one or not. The rhythm does a lot of the work of keeping stakeholders informed, because there's always a demo two weeks out to point people toward.

Kanban doesn't have that boundary, and that's the whole point of it, continuous flow instead of batched commitments, but it means the PM has to manufacture the checkpoints Scrum gave you for free. There's no sprint review to force a "here's what shipped" conversation, so if I don't deliberately create one, stakeholders just stop hearing from me until something's live. There's no sprint commitment to protect scope against, so priority changes can slide in far more casually, which is a feature when priorities genuinely are volatile and a problem when it just means nothing ever gets finished because something newer keeps jumping the queue.

The actual shift I had to make was moving from "protect the sprint" to "protect the WIP limit," which is a much less intuitive thing to defend to a stakeholder. Telling someone "we can't start your thing yet, the sprint is already committed" lands as normal. Telling someone "we can't start your thing yet, we're at our work-in-progress limit" gets you a confused look the first several times, because it's a less familiar constraint even though it's arguably a better one, since it's tied to actual throughput rather than an arbitrary two-week box.

Where I'd measure whether the switch was working: cycle time variance. Scrum hides cycle time inside the sprint box, you mostly see "did we finish the sprint," not how long any one item actually took start to finish. Kanban makes cycle time visible per item, which is more honest but also means a PM who was used to sprint-level reporting has no equivalent number to reach for unless they start tracking it deliberately. I didn't, for about a month, and stakeholder updates got noticeably vaguer during that stretch without me clocking why.

I don't think either is better in the abstract. Scrum's forcing functions are a crutch, but crutches are useful when you'd otherwise skip the walk entirely.
