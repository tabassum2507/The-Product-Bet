---
title: "A roadmap is not a backlog with dates on it"
date: 2026-08-04T21:55:00+05:30
tags: ["product-management", "learning-in-public"]
description: "I once put dates on every backlog item and called it a roadmap, then spent a quarter defending dates nobody should have trusted."
---

Early on I built a roadmap by taking the backlog, sorting it by rough priority, and putting a month next to each item based on how big it looked. It had a start date, an end date, and forty rows. A director in a review asked what would happen to the March commitments if January's item slipped, and I didn't have an answer beyond "everything shifts right," which isn't a plan, it's just admitting the dates were fake from the start.

A roadmap and a backlog answer different questions and I'd conflated them completely. A backlog is a list of things you might build, roughly ordered. A roadmap is supposed to communicate intent and direction to people who aren't going to read the backlog, why you're investing where you're investing, and what tradeoffs that implies, at a level of confidence that's honest about how far out you're looking. Putting specific dates on distant backlog items doesn't make it a roadmap, it makes it a backlog wearing a costume, and it sets you up to either miss dates in public or quietly pad every estimate until the roadmap is meaningless in the other direction.

The version that's worked better for me is a roadmap organized by theme and rough horizon, now, next, later, with specific dates only in the "now" column, where I actually have enough certainty to commit to one. "Next" gets a quarter, not a date. "Later" gets a sentence about direction and nothing else. This annoyed a sales lead who wanted a specific month for something in "later" so she could tell a prospect, and I understand the pressure that creates, but giving her a fake date doesn't actually help her, it just moves the awkward conversation to whenever that date slips instead of having it now, up front, honestly.

Where I think this connects to the interview prep I've been doing: a roadmap is really a commitment device, and the tradeoff is precision versus trust. A precise roadmap that's wrong damages trust more than a vague one that's honest about its own uncertainty, because people remember being told something specific and false more than they remember being told "we're not sure yet." I'd rather take the short-term discomfort of an executive asking why I can't give a date than the longer damage of missing three dates in a row on a document people thought was a commitment.

If I were checking whether a roadmap is actually functioning as one, I'd look at how often it changes and whether people are surprised when it does. A backlog changing weekly is normal. A roadmap changing weekly means it was never really a roadmap, just a backlog snapshot someone printed and called strategy.
