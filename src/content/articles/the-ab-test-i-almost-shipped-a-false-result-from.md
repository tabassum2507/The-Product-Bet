title: "The A/B test I almost shipped a false result from"
date: 2026-09-02T20:37:00+05:30
tags: ["analytics", "interview-prep"]
description: "I checked a test's results every morning and nearly called a winner three days early, off a lead that vanished by the time the test actually finished."
---

Three days into a two-week A/B test on the churn-rescue agent's opening line, variant B was ahead by a wide enough margin that I drafted a message announcing we'd found a winner and could roll it out early. I didn't send it, mostly out of a vague unease rather than any principled statistical reasoning, and by the end of the full run the gap had almost entirely closed, landing well within what looked like noise. If I'd shipped on day three, I'd have shipped a false result with total confidence.

What I was doing, without knowing the term for it at the time, is called peeking, checking a test's results before it reaches its planned sample size and treating an early lead as meaningful. Early in a test, small sample sizes produce noisy results, and if you check daily, you will eventually catch a day where the noise happens to look like a strong, clean signal, purely by chance, not because anything real is happening. The math behind why this specifically inflates false positives is more precise than I can casually derive, but the intuition stuck with me once I had it: the more times you peek and could stop, the more chances you're giving randomness to fool you, so a test design that assumes you'll only look once, at the end, breaks if you're actually checking every morning and willing to call it early whenever the numbers look good.

The other thing that changed how I read test results is understanding what statistical significance is actually claiming, and isn't. A significant result at the standard threshold means that if there were truly no difference between variants, you'd see a gap this large or larger by chance only some small percentage of the time, it's a statement about how surprising the data would be under no-effect, not a statement about how large or important the effect actually is. A test can be statistically significant and practically meaningless, a real but tiny difference in a metric nobody would reprioritize a roadmap over, and treating significance as synonymous with importance is a separate mistake from peeking, but one I've watched a room make just as often.

The tradeoff underneath all of this is speed against reliability, waiting for a properly powered sample size feels slow when a business wants an answer this week, and there's real organizational pressure to call things early. What I do now is set the sample size and stopping point before the test starts, write it down somewhere visible, and treat any urge to call it early as a signal to check that number, not override it. It's a small discipline, and it's the only thing standing between me and shipping exactly the false result I almost shipped that week, off a lead that would've evaporated four days later.
