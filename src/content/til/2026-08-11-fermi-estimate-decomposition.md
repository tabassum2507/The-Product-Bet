description: "In an estimation round the interviewer cut me off mid-calculation to ask about my breakdown, not my final number."
---

Got a classic Fermi question in a practice round, estimate how many voice AI support calls happen in India per day, and I started multiplying numbers as fast as I could to get to an answer before the clock ran out. The interviewer stopped me about halfway through and asked me to back up and explain why I'd chosen those specific factors to multiply, not to give the final number faster.

That reset what I thought was being graded. I'd assumed the point was landing on a plausible number, something in the right order of magnitude. It's actually the decomposition, whether you break an unknowable question into pieces that are each individually more knowable, population, percentage using relevant services, average contacts per user per year, and whether your reasoning for each piece is at least defensible, even if the final number is off by a factor of two or three.

A wrong final number built on a sensible, clearly-reasoned breakdown is a better answer than a lucky right number you can't explain the path to. I'd been optimizing for the wrong half of the exercise every time I practiced this kind of question alone, because when you're alone nobody stops you to ask why, so you never notice you've been skipping the part that actually mattered.
